With the assembled chassis weighing **566 g** on its **5 V bench supply** and twelve
MG90S servos, October 13–14 went into fixing how Ainekio V2 wakes up on the floor, holds
the body level on uneven surfaces, updates over Wi-Fi without cracking the shell open,
and reacts when picked up or tipped over.

## Staged startup rise (`2.5 s` crouch → shoulder align → stand)

Up to now, booting drove all twelve servos straight toward calibration Home. On a desk
or floor, that could kick the lower legs outward before the hip-roll servos had squared
up the shoulders. Normal startup on the ESP32-P4 now runs a smooth three-stage rise
(leaving calibration Home as a separate bench maintenance command): the legs first tuck
into a compact crouch, the four shoulders align while unloaded, and the front and rear
leg pairs press up into Stand with a **100 ms** stagger over **2.5 seconds**.

## IMU self-leveling controls and integral hold

The ESP32-P4 now stores persistent IMU self-leveling settings in non-volatile memory,
independent of joint and gyro calibration:

| Control | Function |
| --- | --- |
| On / Off | Enables attitude correction in supported standing and walking states |
| Response strength | Gain multiplier for roll/pitch attitude error (default `1×`) |
| Damping | Gain multiplier for gyro angular-rate damping (default `1×`) |
| Roll target | Desired roll offset in degrees (positive raises left side, default `0°`) |
| Pitch target | Desired pitch offset in degrees (positive raises nose, default `0°`) |

Bench tilt checks quickly exposed why proportional-only feedback felt spongy while
standing: under gravity, the legs settled partway short of horizontal. Adding an
integral hold term while stationary lets the legs keep pushing against a sustained tilt
until the torso comes all the way back to its target angle.

### Linkage torque check: 55/35/38/28/20 mm vs 45/35/30/28/20 mm

Before printing any more leg revisions, I wrote a quick kinematic torque script to check
whether shortening the lower leg and coupler rod would ease servo load. Comparing the
installed **55 / 35 / 38 / 28 / 20 mm** linkage (lower leg / upper carrier / connecting
rod / pickup / crank) against a shorter **45 / 35 / 30 / 28 / 20 mm** candidate at a
65 mm hip height showed that while carrier peak torque dropped ~3%, the shorter 30 mm
rod hit a steeper transmission angle near full stand and **pushed knee-crank torque up
by ~16%**. That kept the 55 mm leg and 38 mm rod locked in as the better mechanical fit.

## Onboard SD video recording, Wi-Fi OTA updates, and crash logs

Once the shell panels go on, plugging a USB-C cable into the buried ESP32-P4 header gets
old fast. The P4 now handles video capture, firmware updates, and crash diagnostics
entirely over Wi-Fi:

<figure>
  <img src="/posts/2026-10-14-progress/recording-and-updates.svg" alt="One encoded camera image feeds both the existing preview and SD recorder. Body Control retrieves clips and logs, and uploads firmware for separate explicit activation." width="1000" height="640" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Shared hardware JPEG pipeline for live preview and SD recording, paired with rollback-safe Wi-Fi OTA updates and remote log retrieval.</figcaption>
</figure>

The camera task feeds hardware-encoded JPEGs straight to both the live preview stream
and an onboard SD card writer, saving 60-second (or 64 MiB) MJPEG AVI segments with
zero re-encoding and rolling over the oldest clip when the card fills. Uploading new
firmware over Wi-Fi drops the robot into a safe resting crouch, closes any active video
segment, writes the inactive flash slot, and waits for an explicit reboot—rolling back
automatically in the bootloader if the new build fails to come up and authenticate with
the gateway. Current and prior-boot crash logs can also be pulled straight from the
browser.

### 469-second Wi-Fi stability and concurrent recording run

Keeping the ESP32-C6 Wi-Fi coprocessor out of power-save sleep, riding through brief
TCP ACK stalls instead of tearing down the socket, and serializing WebSocket transmits
cured the intermittent link drops from earlier bench runs:

| Metric | Hardware verification run |
| --- | --- |
| Continuous session window | **469 s** (235 status samples, 1 connection epoch, **0 reconnects**) |
| Concurrent preview + SD recording | VGA at 1 fps; **411 frames** across 8 finalized AVI segments |
| Frame drops | **0 recording drops**, **0 camera-queue drops** |
| Concurrent clip download | Downloaded a **2.88 MB** 60 s MJPEG AVI while streaming and recording continued |
| Peak TCP ACK delay survived | **1,270 ms** recovered cleanly on the same socket |

## Body Event Trigger: wake, tipped over, and rapid movement

Physical disturbances on the bench now kick off real reactions instead of going
unnoticed. IMU attitude and boot/wake transitions route from Body Control into a
dedicated **Body Event Trigger** workflow in MetaHuman OS, debounced into single
episodes so a bumped robot doesn't spam itself:

<figure>
  <img src="/posts/2026-10-14-progress/body-events.svg" alt="P4 measurements pass through Body Control and the Environment Bridge to Body Event Trigger. Its model can take no action or delegate to Environment Mode, which sends selected actions back to the body." width="1000" height="640" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>IMU tilt, sudden acceleration, and wake transitions fire episode-debounced events into MetaHuman OS.</figcaption>
</figure>

| Event | Trigger threshold | Clearing / debounce behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wake** | Boot, sleep-wake, or manual *Good morning* button | Evaluates wake intention (greet, capture photo, stretch, stand) |
| **Tipped over** | Tilt `> 60°` for `300 ms` | Single episode until tilt drops below `45°` |
| **Rapid movement** | Accel `|a − g| > 0.75 g` or gyro `> 180°/s` | Single episode until below `0.35 g` and `90°/s`, gated by a **10 s** cooldown |

## Teaching the Task model to separate quoted speech from movement

Testing those body events on the live robot turned up a funny failure mode: when a
shake event asked for a dry remark followed by a quick shrug, the 0.8B Task model read
the words inside the suggested spoken line as a literal movement command and started
walking sideways in **Crab** instead.

I trained two contrast LoRA candidates (4,350 and 4,926 examples) pairing movement
idioms in dialogue—like *"Take a bow, my friend"*—with unrelated physical gestures:

<figure>
  <img src="/posts/2026-10-14-progress/task-qualification.svg" alt="On 1,221 evaluation cases, acceptable reference matches rose from 533 to 1,011. Historical regression tests still showed three missed actions versus zero, so the new candidate was not deployed." width="1000" height="640" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Evaluation accuracy jumped from 43.7% to 82.8%, but 3 missed actions on the historical regression suite held back deployment—pointing directly to the October 15 architectural split.</figcaption>
</figure>

| Frozen benchmark metric | Deployed Task adapter | Second contrast candidate |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Evaluation reference matches | 533 / 1,221 (43.7%) | **1,011 / 1,221 (82.8%)** |
| Evaluation wrong physical actions | 313 | **94** |
| Evaluation unintended physical actions | 125 | **50** |
| Historical regression matches | 82 / 141 | **110 / 141** |
| Historical missed physical actions | **0** | 3 |

Forcing a tiny 0.8B action model to carry spoken dialogue inside its action JSON was the
wrong boundary in the first place. That realization set up the cleaner fix in the
[October 15 companion systems update](/posts/2026-10-15-companion-systems/): stripping
speech out of the Task planner altogether so **Persona** owns every spoken word.
([Download the October 14 Task comparison JSON](/posts/2026-10-14-progress/task-model-comparison.json).)
