Between October 25 and October 27, work across the ESP32-P4 firmware, power packaging,
and the Freestyle motion generator converged on real closed-loop movement: curing a
nasty motion-task stack crash, weighing the complete **650 g** robot with its rear 2S
power carrier and bench calibration stand, switching Freestyle from twitchy open-ended
continuation loops to **one-shot finite target sequences** verified by the 4th-wire
potentiometers, and keeping multi-step tasks alive across turns.

## 650 g physical build, rear 2S power carrier, and compact calibration stand

Putting the fully assembled wireless robot on the scale brought the real hardware in at
**650 g** (up from 566 g on October 13 before the shell panels, harness, and power
carrier went on), spanning **245.85 × 182.79 × 109.06 mm** in neutral stance. Both
earlier USB power banks are gone for good—replaced by a compact one-piece rear carrier
holding a **2S LiPo pack, Mini560 5 V / 5 A buck regulator, and HiLetgo 2S USB-C boost
charger**:

| Assembled robot mass | **650 g** |
| 2S battery pack envelope | **73.6 × 46.0 × 22.0 mm** (8.4 V full charge) |
| Mini560 5 V / 5 A buck converter | **30.0 × 18.0 × 5.6 mm** (dedicated 5 V servo rail) |
| HiLetgo 2S USB-C charger | **36.5 × 17.75 × 1.5 mm** bare PCB (6.8 mm overall height) |

| Shoulder spacing (fore-aft × left-right) | **173.434 × 63.500 mm** |

### Compact 3-piece calibration stand (`87%` less print volume)

To run standing and walking checks on the desk with all four feet clear of the surface,
I replaced the bulky clamp fixture with a 3-piece printed stand (**140 × 110 mm** base,
**77.1 mm** overall height) whose two **4.0 × 40.0 mm** blades key directly into the
belly vent slots (42 mm center-to-center). Print volume dropped from `440.8 cm³` to
**`58.0 cm³` (−87%)**.

## Fixing the ESP32-P4 motion stack crash and waiting for real shaft arrival

Triggering `Stand` or `Walk` on an earlier test build suddenly started rebooting the
ESP32-P4. Checking FreeRTOS high-water marks caught the culprit: temporary candidate
structures inside the motion starter were blowing past the 16 KiB task stack. Moving
that scratch buffer into static task-owned storage cut peak stack use more than in
half—from **8,496 bytes down to 3,760 bytes**—letting the robot cycle cleanly through
`Stand` and `Walk` on the bench stand without resetting or dropping Wi-Fi.

With the stack solid, I moved `Stand`, `Walk`, and Freestyle steps from blind timer
playback to **verified 4th-wire shaft arrival**. Lift and placement landmarks now wait
for fresh potentiometer confirmation (within a **100 µs** tolerance) plus a **2 mm
lift-and-return settle** step. Each Freestyle waypoint resolves its inverse-kinematics
target once for both ramping and arrival checks; if a requested foot position has to be
clamped to stay inside the four-bar workspace, the controller reports the clamped delta
honestly instead of claiming the unadjusted target was reached.

## Freestyle: from twitchy continuation loops to finite programs (`349` curated examples)

Freestyle uses a compact **Qwen3.5-0.8B LoRA (Q8_0)** to turn natural-language movement
requests and live posture telemetry into concrete joint, foot, and body targets.

### Compact prompt encoding and off-thread IK preparation

Trimming verbose JSON keys out of the Freestyle prompt cut Q6A CPU inference time by
**4×**, and moving inverse-kinematics preparation off the gateway's main loop cut sway
execution latency in half:

| Optimization | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Q6A CPU prompt / output tokens (happy + 10 mm foot lift) | 3,154 in / 91 out | **823 in / 49 out** |
| Q6A CPU generation time | 72.89 s | **17.90 s** (10.23 s warm) |
| Host IK preparation + 2.8 s rear sway execution | 9.96 s | **5.03 s** (2.54 s for 1.28 s sway) |
| Workstation Q8_0 LoRA API latency | — | **0.663 s** |

### Why continuation loops had to go

On October 26 I tried feeding every finished movement receipt right back into the model
so it could decide whether to keep going. On the physical robot, that turned into an
annoying feedback loop: the model kept re-emitting subtle Stand adjustments and
twitching its legs between steps.

On October 27 I dropped model-driven continuation altogether. Freestyle now emits a
complete finite multi-step sequence and runs it once; when the final waypoint verifies
arrival, the gesture is done. Only an actual physical fault—a leg stalling under load,
a dropped feedback wire, or the IMU catching a tip-over—interrupts execution, holding
powered support in place while requesting a targeted recovery sequence without losing
track of the active movement goal.

Curating the training library down to **349 hand-checked and contrast examples** lifted
agreement on the frozen 62-case benchmark to **40/62** (**24/34** on core motions, up
from 20/34). After fixing a left-crank Home offset (`−117.68° at 2750 µs`), the robot
ran a 9-step Freestyle stretch on the stand with **all 9 steps verified by 4th-wire
feedback** and zero clamped joints.

## Keeping multi-step tasks alive and unloading idle local models

<figure>
  <img src="/posts/2026-10-26-progress/companion-continuity.svg" alt="Diagram of MetaHuman Full Auto continuity, atomic Bridge snapshot publication, and on-demand llama.cpp weight loading." width="1000" height="600" loading="lazy" />

</figure>

On the host side, local `llama.cpp` model serving now loads weights on demand and
automatically unloads them after **5 minutes (300 seconds)** of quiet time so GPU and
system memory aren't tied up while the robot sleeps on the desk. Multi-step autonomous
tasks also stay attached to their active goal across wake triggers instead of starting
over from scratch, bridge state snapshots write atomically so fast readers never catch a
half-written file, and open-ended conversational questions wait quietly in an idle state
until someone answers.
