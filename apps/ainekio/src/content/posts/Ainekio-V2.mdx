# Building the next Ainekio body

*Cover: September 23 Blender motion-export frame with the red mask and carapace.
Later battery and 80 mm support edits are separate modeling work.*

Ainekio is a small four-legged robot. Its working V1 body uses eight servos,
with two motors positioning each leg. V2 adds a third joint per leg, a new
chassis, and an ESP32-P4 controller. The aim is a twelve-servo body that moves
reliably and is easy to service.

The extra joint matters because it moves a foot sideways. V1’s legs primarily
move forward, backward, up, and down. V2 will also be able to change its stance
width and use that movement in poses. The current controller combines continuous
gait planning with prepared gestures.
Automatic balance and adaptation to uneven ground require further sensing,
control and physical testing.

## Current revision: September 24

The body now has compact mask screw mounts, a tapered rear plate and reinforced
center-support tabs following reported failures in printed parts. The latest
modeling scene measures **80 mm across the exterior support walls**, while the
spanning plates and animation layouts retain the earlier revision. This remains
an assembly in development, with alignment and clearance work outstanding.

The underside is being fitted around the **10,000 mAh Anker PowerCore Slim**
already on hand. The removable bank has integrated charging and uses no separate
buck converter; a plug-in Qi charging add-on is planned. Runtime has not been
measured. Its latest placement has a one-pose contact check, separate from the
earlier broader motion study.

## Legs and chassis

*Earlier design direction, retained for the build history.*

The early V2 leg plan had **hip roll** for sideways movement, **hip pitch** for
forward and backward movement, and a **knee** that bends the leg. Together they
provide more foot-placement options and room for body lean and expressive
poses. Speed is not the first priority.

OpenHarmony Puppy V2 provides a reference for lightweight MG90S legs, supported
pivots, and linkages that keep motor weight close to the body. Local files
include printable STL models and STEP assemblies converted for Blender.
Testing will establish how those ideas fit Ainekio’s shorter, broader shape.

The chassis is being assembled in Blender around the leg mounts, board models,
connectors, and front camera/display carrier. The original OpenHarmony-derived
assembly has since been revised through the
[direct-rod and compact-leg work](/posts/2026-09-21-compact-leg-design/).
The [September 9 short-rod study](/posts/2026-09-09-leg-mechanism-study/)
remains a separate historical comparison of alternatives.

Cables need clearance throughout a leg’s movement. Programming, charging, power
connections, legs, and electronics should remain accessible for replacement
and repair without dismantling the whole body.

revision’s layout; it is not a wiring diagram.*

`Robot Layout → LeftRightSpacing_mm` controls the leg roots, center mounts,
inserted panel sections, covers, and later face mounting positions. At the
97 mm setting, the covers are 112 mm wide. Widening moves the existing mounting
features rather than scaling their holes. The cover and face checks also
exercised the 107 mm setting.

The WIP 5 layout provides a 6 mm gap behind the Q6A tray and between the rear
board envelopes. Four tray cutouts include 0.3 mm around modeled connectors.
Those clearances belong to that saved layout. Real plugs, cable bends, cooling,
and full leg motion need a separate check; the tray still overlaps the later
panel and bottom cover and requires trimming.

## Controller and motion: earlier plan and September progress

The paragraphs below preserve the earlier controller plan. By September 17,
the project had a separate twelve-joint model and thirty geometric motions,
and the selected signal driver was a HUAREW PCA9685 breakout. That hardware
selection is distinct from the Adafruit board model in the September 10 CAD
table. The [integration record](/posts/2026-09-21-design-progress/data/motion-integration-record.txt)
reports a built motion image, pending flash and unavailable physical motion.

ESP32-S3 and ESP32-P4 name different controller chip families. Choosing the P4
does not make the existing firmware ready for the new body. Board pins,
communication hardware, camera, and audio still need their own implementation
and tests; V1’s S3 wiring map is not a P4 wiring guide.

The original plan favored twelve servo-control signals generated directly by
the P4. The newer CAD includes a PCA9685 pulse-width modulation (PWM) board.
Both approaches still require pin allocation, control software, calibration,
and peripheral tests; a board model is not evidence that either path is wired.

A scripted motion consists of frames. Each frame specifies a target angle for
each joint and how long the transition should take. For V2, a frame will contain
twelve targets. Interpolation supplies the positions between frames so the
robot moves smoothly instead of jumping from one pose to the next.

Planned controls include center calibration, direction inversion, minimum and
maximum angles, velocity and acceleration limits, safe rest poses, and staged
startup and stand-up sequences. Existing movements can first be adapted with
the four new lateral joints held neutral, then use those joints selectively.

Walk, stop, stand, sit, bow, and wave remain examples of higher-level requests.
At the initial planning stage, the body core, motion assets, protocol validators,
and MetaHuman motion-plan schema described eight joints. They needed to agree on the new joint
names, limits, and advertised capabilities before accepting twelve-joint plans.

## Power: measure the load before choosing the supply

The owner reports V1 operating on its existing 5 V / 5 A supply. The design note
proposes measuring that robot first, then evaluating a 5–6 A supply line, or
rail, under V2 loads. Four extra servos mean the V1 result cannot establish the
new robot’s requirements.

Controller and servo power will branch near the supply. Motor current will
bypass the P4 board, while a common ground gives the controller and servos the
same electrical reference. A bulk capacitor near the servo rail can help with
brief current demands; the supply still has to sustain the load. Smooth
acceleration and staged movement will be part of testing.

A battery or power bank must deliver the needed current at 5 V continuously,
cope with startup and motion peaks, and remain stable in voltage and
temperature. An advertised USB-PD wattage does not by itself establish those
properties. The final power source remains undecided.

## Build and test sequence

1. **Make one leg.** Measure fit, servo horn alignment, travel, interference,
   stiffness, bearing support, printed weight, and reachable foot positions.
   The horn is the arm on the servo shaft; backlash is unwanted joint play.
   Both affect how accurately a printed assembly follows a commanded angle.
2. **Measure V1 power use and adjust the leg design.** Use the existing robot
   and the experimental leg to inform the mechanical and electrical choices.
3. **Test the P4 in stages.** Check signals and calibration, then one loaded
   leg, two legs, and all twelve servos.
4. **Validate the modeled chassis and design the wiring harness.** Check them
   against measured parts and power needs, with room for motion and service access.
5. **Port scripted movements.** Tune transitions before the final complete print.
6. **Repeat normal movements under load.** Test standing, sitting, walking,
   turning, and stopping. Watch for binding, supply shutdown, brownouts
   (voltage drops that reset electronics), and frequent near-stall operation.
   Check controller capacity and repair access as well.

## Later options

Adaptive terrain response, foot-contact sensing, and closed-loop balance—
adjusting movement from sensor feedback—are deferred. So are stabilization
with an inertial sensor (IMU), calculating joint angles from desired foot
positions in real time (inverse kinematics), and learned locomotion.
Higher-cost servos and wireless charging were outside the initial build plan.
Onboard Linux computing was also excluded from that first scope. The Q6A model
records the September 10 wide-body packaging study; its board and tray were
retired from the September 17 narrow chassis. No onboard Q6A runtime is
established by either CAD layout.

The [MetaHuman redesign](/posts/metahuman-integration/) can develop against V1
