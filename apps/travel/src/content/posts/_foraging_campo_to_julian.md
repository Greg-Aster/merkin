# Foraging Campo to Julian: A Field Guide to the First 77 Miles

The first 77 miles of the Pacific Crest Trail span two distinct ecological zones before reaching Julian. Understanding where the terrain shifts — and what grows in each zone — determines what's actually available to forage and when.

This is not a calorie strategy. It's a supplement. Know what you're looking at and pick it up when it's there.

---

## The Terrain

**Miles 0–41 | Chaparral Scrubland (~2,900–6,000 ft)**

The trail begins at the southern terminus near Campo at roughly 2,900 feet and climbs steadily through classic Southern California chaparral — dense, dry-adapted shrubs, exposed hillsides, and rocky drainages. This is not true desert. Chaparral is defined by its shrub cover and semi-arid Mediterranean climate: wet winters, dry summers, infrequent fires. It looks sparse but supports a variety of edible plants, particularly in spring.

By mile 41, the trail reaches Mount Laguna at around 6,000 feet. The vegetation shifts noticeably — pine trees appear, the air cools, and the chaparral scrub gives way to a mountain forest mix.

**Miles 41–77 | High Desert Transition and San Felipe Valley (~3,000–6,000 ft)**

After Mount Laguna the trail descends. By Scissors Crossing at mile 77 (2,276 ft), the terrain has dropped back into open, drier country — the San Felipe Valley. Cactus diversity increases significantly north of Scissors Crossing. This lower stretch is drier, more exposed, and more stereotypically "desert" than the chaparral miles at the start.

**Seasonal window (NOBO March–April):** Early spring hikers catch the tail end of the wet season. Wildflowers are out, yucca begins to flower, and annual greens appear in shaded pockets. Water sources are more reliable than in summer. Daytime temperatures are manageable at lower elevations, but Mount Laguna can see freezing nights and occasional snow in March.

## What's Edible

---

### Prickly Pear *(Opuntia* spp.)

**Where:** Both terrain zones. Most abundant in the lower chaparral and especially north of Scissors Crossing. Found on rocky slopes, open hillsides, and trail margins.

**Season:** Pads are available year-round. Fruit (tuna) ripens late summer through fall — not present on a March/April hike. Early spring hikers will find pads only.

**Edible parts:**
- **Pads (nopales):** The flat, paddle-shaped stems. Harvest young, smaller pads — more tender and fewer developed spines.
- **Fruit:** Deep red-purple when ripe. Not available in spring.

**Prep:** Use a stick, rock, or tongs to hold the pad. Burn off glochids (tiny hair-like spines) over a flame, or scrape them off against a rock. Slice into strips. Eat raw, roast over a fire, or add to hot water for a mucilaginous broth. The slippery texture is normal.

**Nutrition (per 100g raw pad):** ~16 calories, 3.5g carbohydrate, 2g fiber, vitamin C, calcium, magnesium. High water content — useful for hydration in dry stretches.

**Note:** Low caloric density. The value is water content and micronutrients, not calories.

---

### Yucca *(Yucca* spp., likely *Y. whipplei* or *Y. baccata* in this region)

**Where:** Common throughout the chaparral zone, especially on dry, rocky slopes. Unmistakable silhouette — rosette of stiff, sword-like leaves with a tall flowering stalk.

**Season:** Flowers appear March through May. A spring NOBO hike falls squarely in the flowering window. The tall stalk (scape) emerges before the flowers open — this is the most useful part.

**Edible parts:**
- **Flower petals:** Mildly sweet, slightly bitter. Eat raw or cooked.
- **Emerging flower stalk:** Cut before it fully extends. Roast or boil.
- **Roots:** Edible but require significant digging and prolonged cooking to reduce saponins. Not trail-practical.

**Prep:** Petals can be eaten directly off the stalk. For the stalk, cut as low as possible before it elongates, slice into sections, and roast over coals or boil 10–15 minutes. Texture is similar to asparagus.

**Nutrition (petals, per 100g):** ~36 calories, 8g carbohydrate, some vitamin C and calcium. The stalk provides more substantial carbohydrate energy.

**Note:** Timing matters. The stalk is most useful before it elongates — once it's 6+ feet tall, the base becomes too fibrous.

---

### Desert Sage / Chia Sage *(Salvia columbariae — Golden Chia)*

**Where:** Dry, open slopes throughout the chaparral zone. Small blue-purple flowers on erect stalks with wrinkled, aromatic leaves. Identifiable by the strong sage scent when leaves are crushed.

**Season:** Seeds mature late spring through early summer. A March/April hiker may find last season's seed heads still on dried stalks — worth checking.

**Edible parts:**
- **Seeds:** Nutritionally identical to commercial chia. Tiny, grey-black, embedded in dried seed heads.
- **Leaves:** Brewed as tea. Mild and aromatic.

**Prep:** Seeds: Crush dried seed heads between the palms over a container or bandana. Winnow away chaff by blowing gently. Soak seeds in water 15–20 minutes — they form a gel. Add to water, oatmeal, or eat as-is. Tea: Steep a small handful of fresh or dried leaves in hot water for 5 minutes.

**Nutrition (per 28g seeds):** ~138 calories, 5g protein, 9g fat (mostly omega-3), 12g carbohydrate, 11g fiber, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium.

**Note:** Seed collection is slow unless the plant is abundant. The tea costs nothing — make it whenever water is being heated anyway.

---

### Mesquite *(Prosopis* spp.)

**Where:** Lower elevations, dry washes, and valley floors. More common approaching Scissors Crossing and in the San Felipe Valley. Identified by small paired leaflets on arching branches and long, constricted seed pods hanging in clusters.

**Season:** Pods mature summer through fall. Dried pods from the previous season may persist on trees through spring — brown, hard, and still usable.

**Edible parts:** The entire pod (hull and seeds). The sweet, dry flesh surrounds hard seeds.

**Prep:** Chew pods directly — the sweet outer flesh dissolves. Spit out the hard seeds or swallow whole (they pass undigested). For more use: grind dried pods with a rock into coarse flour and mix with water to form a paste. Alternatively, soak broken pods in water a few hours to produce a sweet, nutritious drink. The resulting preparation (mesquite pinole) was a staple food for indigenous peoples of the Sonoran Desert.

**Nutrition (per 100g pod flour):** ~217 calories, 13g protein, 43g carbohydrate, 17g fiber, high in calcium, potassium, zinc, and iron.

**Note:** One of the more nutritionally complete forageable foods in this section. Easy to identify, minimal processing required. When pods are present, pick up a handful.

---

### Miner's Lettuce *(Claytonia perfoliata)* and Wild Mustard *(Brassica* spp.)

**Where:** Shaded, moist pockets — creek margins, north-facing slopes, areas with recent rainfall. Not uniformly distributed. Look for bright green growth in otherwise dry terrain after wet weather.

**Season:** Peak availability March–May. Both are early-season annuals that die back as temperatures rise and moisture disappears.

**Edible parts:** Leaves, stems, and flowers of both species. Entire above-ground plant.

**Prep:** Eat raw. Rinse if practical. Miner's lettuce is mild and palatable without any preparation. Wild mustard leaves are more pungent — younger leaves are milder. Both can be briefly wilted over heat.

**Nutrition:**
- *Miner's lettuce (per 100g):* ~20 calories, high vitamin C, vitamin A, iron, significant water content.
- *Wild mustard greens (per 100g):* ~26 calories, high vitamin C and K, calcium, folate.

**Note:** Presence is patchy and not guaranteed. When found, worth eating. Fresh food and vitamin C have real value on a long haul.

## What to Avoid

**Milky sap:** Any plant that bleeds white latex when broken. Euphorbias are common in warm, dry climates and most are toxic. Avoid entirely.

**Unknown pods:** Mesquite is distinctive — confirm the ID before harvesting. Many legumes native to this region are toxic. When uncertain, leave it.

**Red berries:** Most red berries in Southern California chaparral are toxic or require processing to be safe. Not worth the risk on trail.

**Poodle Dog Bush *(Eriodictyon parryi)*:** Common in burn areas throughout this section. Causes severe contact dermatitis. Learn to identify it to avoid skin contact.
