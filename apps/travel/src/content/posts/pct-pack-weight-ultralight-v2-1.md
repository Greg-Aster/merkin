My pack weighs 30 pounds empty. Loaded, I'll be carrying around 45. I am 215 pounds. There is a lot of pressure online to go ultralight, and I'd like to push back on that a little.

Not everyone needs to go ultralight. Simply shaving pounds for the sake of it can actually be harmful.

## Weather Matters

If you're an early spring hiker, you're going to encounter much colder weather and you need to be prepared for that. The ultralight approach that works in June does not work the same way in March. Pack for the conditions you'll actually face.

## Size Ratio Matters

I am 215 pounds. I can carry a much heavier load than people of lighter weights. The math is just different. A 45 pound pack on me is a different thing than a 45 pound pack on someone half my size.

I also need more calories because of my size. These things scale with the person carrying them.

## Your Body Adapts to What You Train With

Consider this: if I lost 65 pounds overnight, I would have a negative 65 pound weight ratio, and could in theory carry 80 pounds and feel like I was flying. That argument makes no sense until you consider that if you are consistently carrying 80 pounds — like hikers did in the old days — your body would adjust to it just fine. Within reason, of course.

So the real question is: what is your training weight?

If you started the trail tomorrow and picked up a 45 pound bag cold, it would feel very heavy and it would suck. You might even hurt yourself over time. But if you have been hiking with a 45 pound bag regularly and your body has adapted, there is no issue. Your body is just accustomed to the pack weight.

If you consider this, then people who are ultralighting at 15 pounds and below are simply training themselves on lower weights. If they were going from the couch to the trail, they would do much better with a lighter pack. But for an experienced hiker who has put in the miles with a heavier load, comfort is key. A cushy bag, reliable gear, a decent pillow, plenty of food. That strategy, in the long run, is actually smarter than shaving off as many pounds as possible and being underprepared just for the sake of a number.

## The Cost of Going Light

Another issue with ultralight packing is the expense of the gear. Quality gear matters — you are going to intimately spend a lot of time sleeping and living in what you carry. The more expensive gear tends to be lighter, yes, but there is also sometimes a trade-off. People with lighter packs often have more breakages and have to purchase more to get by.

Thru-hiking should be accessible to everyone, poor and rich alike. Nature is a resource that does not require capitalist ventures. We are not trying to buy our way through the wilderness or give in to the latest gear fads.

Be less miserable. Be comfortable. Enjoy yourself. Just make sure you are properly trained for what you carry, that you can afford what you have, and that you can afford to fix it when it breaks.

## The Guy on the Mountain

When I climbed Mount Hood I was wearing a cotton army surplus jacket, jeans, and work boots. Every other hiker on the mountain that day was wearing synthetic, hiker-specific, expensive gear. I stuck out like a sore thumb. Other hikers didn't really want to have anything to do with me, but that was fine.

I made it to the top and back down and had a very good time.

I think a lot of the people climbing that day didn't consider that you could just get off your couch and do it — if you are healthy and capable. Instead, people tend to go out and buy gear, prep extensively, train, join climbing clubs, tie themselves to each other. It is quite a sight to see four guys in extreme mountaineering gear, decked out for Everest, while a guy in a jacket, jeans, and boots just walks by.

Anyway. These are just thoughts. Pack what works for you, train for what you carry, and don't let anyone with a titanium spork make you feel bad about it.
